### RLG-151 — Stars belong to the clear night sky, and a tundra has a small chance of an aurora
- **RULED 2026-09-01T20:16:22-04:00** — Owner, 2026-09-01: "Can the clear night sky have stars? The tundra should have a small chance
to have an aurora borealis."

THE SKY ALREADY HAS STARS, WHICH CHANGES WHAT THE FIRST HALF IS ASKING. `drawSky` puts 40 of
them out once `nightFall` passes 0.25, each twinkling against `dist`. So the question is not
"add stars" - it is that the owner has been driving at night and has not been reading them as
stars. Three candidates, and they are all cheap to check before anything is built:

    THEY ARE NOT GATED ON THE CLOUD. `cloud` is drawn after them, so heavy cover does paint
    over them - but their own alpha does not know about it, so a clear night and a half
    overcast one put out the same forty. The owner's word is CLEAR, and that is the lever:
    stars should be a property of there being nothing in the way.

    FORTY IS FEW, and their placement is a lattice - `(i * 137.5) % W` across and `(i * 61) %
    (horizon * 0.72)` down. On a 480-pixel screen that is a regular scatter of single pixels,
    which reads as noise rather than as a sky.

    AND THE BLOOM SITS OVER THEM. The horizon glow is painted after the stars at 0.35 of its
    strength even at midnight, so the lower sky - where a driver is actually looking - is
    washed before the stars are seen.

MEASURE WHICH ONE IT IS BEFORE CHANGING ANY OF THEM. A capture at `--hour 0.25` with the cover
pinned clear, and the same with it pinned overcast, answers it in two pictures. RLG-041 is the
standing lesson about diagnosing a device report from a guess.

THE AURORA IS THE NEW THING, AND IT IS A MOMENT RATHER THAN A PLACE. A tundra is a place and it
is always there; an aurora is not, so it belongs with the things that are ROLLED for a stretch
rather than stated in the table - the same shape the sea's side, the crop's field and the
window's habit all have. One roll, held for a run of road, read by the painter.

    WHAT IT NEEDS FROM THE OWNER, and it is one question: how small is "a small chance"? A
    tundra is one place in eleven and a run passes through a handful of places, so a 1-in-10
    aurora is a thing a player might see once in twenty runs. That may be too rare to be worth
    building or exactly rare enough to be worth seeing - the owner's call, and RLG-142's
    temperature step was blocked on the same kind of question.

    AND IT IS A NIGHT THING ONLY. It has to ride `nightFall` the way the stars do, or a green
    curtain appears over a lit tundra at noon.

    IT SHOULD ALSO WANT A CLEAR SKY, for the same reason the stars do - which makes the two
    halves of this note one mechanism seen twice, and is the argument for doing them together.

WHERE IT GOES. `drawSky` after the stars and before the bloom, so the bloom washes it the way
it washes everything else at the horizon. The canyon's continuous skyline shows the shape a
band drawn across the sky takes; an aurora is that walk again with a colour ramp and a slow
drift, and [[RLG-057]] already holds the owner's ask for a sky that is a range rather than a
switch.
- **RULED 2026-09-08T05:16:15-04:00** — BUILT 2026-09-08, v0.13.36. Both halves, and the ruling's instruction to MEASURE FIRST was
followed and changed what got built.

THE MEASUREMENT, which is the part worth keeping. At midnight with the cover pinned, counting
points brighter than their own row: 2,499 on a clear sky and 2,580 under nine tenths cover. THE
COUNT DOES NOT FALL WHEN THE SKY CLOSES OVER - the same sky twice - and reading the code says why:
the star alpha never mentioned `cloud` at all. That is the ruling's FIRST candidate and it is the
owner's own word, CLEAR. The alpha is gated on the cover now.

THE THIRD CANDIDATE WAS ALREADY FIXED AND THE FRAGMENT DID NOT KNOW IT. It said the horizon bloom
sits over the stars at 0.35 of its strength even at midnight; the bloom is driven purely by `gold`
now and reaches zero at night, so nothing is washing them. The fragment was written before that
change. Worth noting as a general point: a ruling that names a mechanism is a snapshot of the code
on the day it was written.

THE SECOND CANDIDATE IS A JUDGMENT AND IT WAS TAKEN. Forty single pixels on a regular lattice read
as dither rather than as a sky. There are 110 now, at three brightnesses and two sizes, with the
lattice broken by a per-star offset - a sky is not evenly spaced, and evenly spaced is what the old
one was. A CLEAR SKY ALSO HAS NO CLOUD IN IT NOW: the puff count had a floor of three, so the
lightest cover a place rolls still put three banks up and a clear night was never actually clear.

THE AURORA. Rolled once when a place opens, held for that place, and drawn after the stars and
before the bloom so the sky treats it as sky. It is three bands, each a wave in x with its own
height and phase, filled from a colour at the top to nothing at the bottom - the light hangs DOWN
from a ragged upper edge and fades before it reaches anything, which is what separates an aurora
from a stripe of green paint. Green low, violet above it. It drifts on `dist` and on real time at
very different rates so it never repeats and never twitches.

ONE TUNDRA IN FOUR, and that is the ruling's open question answered as decided-for-now because the
queue was to be run without checking back. The ruling worked the sum the other way and worried that
one in ten would be seen once in twenty runs; a tundra is one place in eleven and a run passes a
handful of places, so a quarter is roughly one run in a dozen. Measured through the engine's own
roll: 25.5%, 26.2%, 28.5% over 400 openings, and zero in a desert or a forest.

IT ALSO WANTS A CLEAR NIGHT, which is what makes the two halves one mechanism rather than two
features. Measured: green at 0.124 of the sky on a clear tundra night and 0.0000 under cloud.

AND THE FIRST PLACEMENT COULD NOT BE SEEN. The curtain hung at a tenth of the way down the sky,
which on a phone is directly behind the rear-view mirror. It was drawn correctly and was invisible.

### What the harness proves, and one thing it does not

`tools/aurora-test.py` asserts the AURORA - green where there was none, gone under cloud, one tundra
in four, nowhere else - and every one of those falsifies: with the curtain not drawn the green lines
go red.

IT DOES NOT PROVE THE STAR GATE, and that is printed in the output rather than hidden. Run against a
build with the gate taken back out, the point ordering still held - 65 clear against 48 at half
cover - because cloud is drawn OVER a star as well as dimming it, so a count cannot tell the two
apart. An assertion that cannot fail on a broken engine is worse than no assertion, so it is a
diagnostic. Separating them would need the star layer captured on its own, which the engine has no
way to do.

THREE THINGS ABOUT THE INSTRUMENT COST A MEASUREMENT EACH, and all three are the same shape - the
number was real and was about something else. MIDNIGHT IS PHASE 0.25, not 0; a first attempt read a
lit afternoon with a sky mean of 100. THE REAR-VIEW MIRROR is a bright rectangle full of road inside
the sampled band and put thousands of "points" into every reading. AND THE HORIZON MOVES with the
road's relief, which is generated per run, so a band measured from it counted a different amount of
treeline every time - the same nominal sky read 187 points on one run and 484 on the next.
